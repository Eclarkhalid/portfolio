## Problem and constraints

Ryterr turns a topic into an article that a website can publish. The initial
product question looked simple: how should an AI system write a useful post?

The production question was broader. The system had to research current sources,
carry evidence into the draft, review claims, generate supporting assets, respect
a brand voice, preserve an owned content format and deliver the approved result
to different kinds of websites.

Each stage can fail independently. A source can disappear. A claim can remain
unsupported. Image generation can fail while the article is still valid. A CMS
can reject an otherwise approved post. Billing and credits must also remain
consistent if the pipeline stops halfway through.

## Architecture

I structured Ryterr as a staged pipeline rather than a single generation call.
The major stages cover topic and search analysis, research, outline, draft,
search structure, images, fact checking, quality review and persistence.

The pipeline carries structured state forward. Research evidence informs the
draft. Fact checking can identify and repair exact claims. Quality checks can
report a specific weakness. Persistence stores the final article together with
its review state and generated assets.

Publishing is a separate layer. The reviewed post has a stable public contract
and can move through webhooks, a read-only Content API, WordPress, Ghost or
GitHub. Markdown remains the canonical owned format, with sanitized HTML for
systems that need rendered content.

Delivery attempts have their own state and retry path. A temporary publishing
failure does not require the article to be researched and generated again.

## Alternatives I rejected

I rejected a one-shot prompt as the core architecture. It produces text quickly,
but it provides weak control over source use, review, recovery and product
feedback.

I also rejected binding the canonical article to one editor or CMS. That would
make publishing convenient for one integration while creating lock-in for every
other destination.

Finally, I kept unshipped connectors out of the active product story. An
integration should not be marketed as available until its authentication,
delivery and failure states are implemented together.

## Hardest decision

The hardest decision was deciding what counted as complete.

If completion meant that prose existed, the pipeline could finish early. If it
meant that every optional asset and remote destination succeeded, one secondary
failure could invalidate useful work.

The system therefore distinguishes the reviewed content artifact from its
delivery state. The article can be complete and owned while a connection records
a recoverable publishing failure.

## Outcome

Ryterr now behaves like a publishing system rather than an isolated writer. The
pipeline exposes meaningful stages, keeps research and review evidence, returns
portable files and records what happened after publication was requested.

That architecture gives users a clearer artifact and gives operators better
failure boundaries. The broader lesson is that an AI product becomes more useful
when it owns the workflow around generation: evidence before the draft, review
after the draft and accountable delivery at the end.
