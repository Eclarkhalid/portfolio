## Problem and constraints

SpyGlow monitors public competitor websites and turns relevant changes into
intelligence. The difficult part is not taking a snapshot. It is deciding whether
a difference represents a real business event.

Web pages are unstable inputs. A capture can be incomplete, blocked by a consent
screen or affected by a temporary rendering problem. Markup can change without
the visible meaning changing. A monitoring product that reports those events as
competitor moves quickly loses trust.

The system also has an economic constraint. Browser work and AI interpretation
both carry cost, so the pipeline cannot treat every fetch and every difference
as equally valuable.

## Architecture

I designed the monitoring path as a sequence of explicit responsibilities.

1. The capture layer retrieves the public page and records the snapshot.
2. A quality layer compares the result with a rolling baseline and identifies
   thin or suspicious captures.
3. A recheck layer gives suspicious results another chance before they become
   accepted events.
4. Deterministic comparison establishes what changed between valid snapshots.
5. AI interpretation receives the evidence and adds a summary, significance and
   useful context.
6. Reports, alerts, AskGlow and evidence-linked battle cards consume the
   validated intelligence.

Scheduled work, browser processes, queues and notifications live around that
core path. Watchdog and recovery behavior make the scheduler observable instead
of assuming recurring work is healthy because a process is running.

## Alternatives I rejected

The shortest implementation was to send every pair of page captures to a model
and ask it to identify the change. I rejected that as the primary comparison
mechanism because it combines evidence collection, diffing and interpretation in
one probabilistic step.

I also rejected treating every small difference as an alert. More alerts can
look like more value in a demo, but noise transfers the review cost to the user.
The product needs to preserve important evidence while filtering changes that do
not affect a competitive decision.

## Hardest decision

The hardest design decision was where AI should enter the pipeline.

Using it early made the implementation look flexible but weakened the ability to
explain failures. Using it only after quality and deterministic gates produced a
cleaner contract: software establishes the event, then AI interprets it.

That boundary also made cost behavior easier to control. Invalid captures and
empty comparisons can stop before an expensive model call.

## Outcome

The resulting architecture keeps the evidence attached to the interpretation.
A report or battle card can point back to the underlying observed change instead
of presenting an unsupported conclusion.

The product can recover from weak captures, avoid spending model calls on events
that deterministic checks can reject and give operators clearer signals about
which stage failed. The lesson extends beyond monitoring: an AI feature becomes
more dependable when deterministic systems qualify its inputs and preserve its
receipts.
