A design system is often described as a component library with a colour
palette. That description covers the visible part. The valuable part is an
agreement about meaning: what a colour says, what a word promises, what a
screen shows while it waits, and who is allowed to change each of those
things.

I built a design system for a dense workforce platform, the kind of product
people use all day to approve requests, track deadlines and fix problems under
time pressure. These are the principles that made it work, and they apply to
any product that has to stay consistent while many people build it.

## Start with meaning, then values

Every system has raw values: hex codes, a radius, a type scale, spacing steps.
Components should never use them directly. Between the raw values and the
components sits a layer of **semantic tokens**, names that describe a job:
`primary`, `border`, `muted-foreground`, `success-soft`, `radius-lg`.

This layer pays for itself quickly:

- **Dark mode becomes a property of the token.** Every semantic token has a
  light value and a dark value, so a component written once works in both.
  A literal `#ffffff` in a feature page is the one thing that breaks it, so
  the rule is simple: feature code uses tokens, never literals.
- **Contrast is solved once.** Text tokens are tuned to hold a 4.5:1 contrast
  ratio on every surface they are allowed to sit on. A developer choosing
  `muted-foreground` gets a readable colour without checking it.
- **Fills and text are separate tokens.** The primary colour that fills a
  button is often too dark to read as link text on a dark background. A
  separate `primary-text` token, lighter in dark mode, keeps links legible
  without touching the button.

## Give status one language

In an operational product, status is the information people scan for most.
It needs one vocabulary that means the same thing on every screen.

<Figure
  src="/images/writing/design-system/status-language.svg"
  alt="A table of five status meanings with a coloured pill for each: Approved in green, Review needed in amber, In progress in blue, Overdue in red and Not started in grey."
  width="1200"
  height="675"
  caption="Five meanings, each with a fixed fill, soft ground and text colour, and always a word."
/>

The rules that keep this language trustworthy:

- **Each meaning owns a family of tokens:** a fill for dots and bars, a soft
  ground for pills, and a text colour that stays readable on that ground.
  Mixing them is where contrast bugs come from, so the pairs are documented
  and the component applies them.
- **Colour reinforces a word.** A status pill always carries its label.
  People with colour vision deficiencies, screen reader users and anyone
  glancing at a printed report all get the same meaning.
- **Derived states are calculated, never stored.** "Overdue" combines a due
  date with an incomplete status. Showing it in red is correct; writing it
  over the stored state would lose the real lifecycle.
- **Destructive red is reserved.** It marks irreversible outcomes and stops.
  A failure the system recovered from is information, and it uses the
  information colour.
- **Raw values never reach the screen.** An API enum such as
  `PENDING_REVIEW` is mapped to a human label in one utility, so every screen
  says "Review needed" the same way.

## Let the brand change and keep the meaning fixed

Products often let a customer apply their own look. That flexibility needs a
clear boundary, or a brand colour ends up competing with status colours.

<Figure
  src="/images/writing/design-system/presets.svg"
  alt="Two panels. The left shows buttons in different brand colours and corner shapes. The right shows status pills and chart colours that stay the same."
  width="1200"
  height="675"
  caption="A preset may change the primary colour and the corner radius. Status and chart colours stay the same everywhere."
/>

In this system, an organization picks a theme preset for its workspace. A
preset can change the primary colour, its readable text variant, the corner
radius and the neutral family. It never changes status, chart or avatar
colours. A green "Approved" means the same thing in every workspace.

Two further rules keep presets safe. Every light override has a matching dark
override, so a preset never leaves a bright surface in dark mode. And a preset
with a bright primary, such as lime or amber, ships with a dark foreground
colour, so button text keeps its contrast.

## Put the words into the system

The words in a product are part of its design. They deserve rules as precise
as the colours, written down where designers and engineers will read them.

The rules that mattered most for a product used under pressure:

- **State things directly.** Write "Three checks failed" in place of a
  roundabout phrasing such as "Not all checks passed". Write "This is a large
  change" and "Most mailboxes sync in under a minute". Direct statements are
  faster to read and harder to misread.
- **Keep plain facts and legal limits exactly as written.** "This cannot be
  undone" is a fact. A disclaimer such as "Verification does not guarantee
  employment" stays word for word, because an affirmative rewrite would widen
  the promise.
- **Keep the tone steady.** Errors, empty states and confirmations are
  written plainly, with no irony or jokes, because the reader may be
  frustrated.
- **Name actions with verbs.** Dialog titles are actions ("Reassign task"),
  and cancel buttons can be specific ("Keep task").
- **Errors say what failed and what to do next.** "Conversations could not be
  loaded." followed by a Try again button.
- **Success confirms the outcome.** "Changes saved" tells the person what
  happened. A bare "Success" does not.

## Design every state, beyond the happy path

A screen spends a surprising share of its life loading, empty, failed or
waiting on an action. Each of those moments needs a designed answer.

<Figure
  src="/images/writing/design-system/states.svg"
  alt="An action flow from Save changes to Saving to either a Changes saved message or an error message, and four collection states: loading placeholders, an empty state with Clear filters, an error with Try again, and a dash for an unknown value."
  width="1200"
  height="675"
  caption="The states that surround every action and every collection, each with a designed answer."
/>

- **Pending states use the active verb.** A button reads "Saving" while it
  works, and only the action in flight is disabled. The rest of the screen
  stays usable.
- **Success messages disappear; error messages stay.** A person might look
  away during a save. An error that vanishes after a few seconds is an error
  they never saw.
- **Empty states name the cause.** "No records match these filters" offers
  Clear filters. A collection with nothing in it yet offers the create
  action.
- **Unknown values show a dash.** If a summary could not load, it reads
  "Summary unavailable", and a missing figure shows a dash ("–"). A zero would state a
  fact that may be false.

## Make restraint a rule

Dense products become noisy one reasonable decision at a time: a shadow on a
card, a border around a table, a coloured heading. A design system can hold
the line with a few explicit rules.

- **Borders separate, shadows lift.** Cards, tables and sidebars use a thin
  border and no shadow. Shadows mark only what floats above the page:
  popovers, menus, sheets and toasts. Elevation then carries meaning.
- **Hierarchy comes from spacing and weight first.** Font weight steps from
  regular body text to semibold headings to bold page titles, and spacing
  groups related things. Extra borders and cards come last.
- **One primary action per group.** The filled button is the main action, and
  everything else uses quieter variants.
- **Figures align.** Money, durations and counts use tabular numerals and
  right alignment, so columns can be compared at a glance.

## Make drift hard

A design system that depends on everyone remembering the rules will drift.
The durable version is enforced by the codebase.

- **One source for every primitive.** Product code imports the shared Button,
  Table and StatusPill. It never copies them into a feature, because a copy
  stops receiving fixes.
- **Checks in continuous integration.** A small script can reject raw table
  markup in product screens, or hard-coded colours in feature code. A failing
  build teaches the rule at the moment it matters.
- **Documentation next to the components.** Each component has a short page
  with its props, its intended use and its limits. The page describes the
  real component, because it is written from the real source.
- **Honest coverage notes.** The documentation lists what it does not cover
  yet. A team that knows a gap exists can plan for it.

## The design principle

Treat a design system as an agreement about meaning. Values serve tokens,
tokens serve components, and components serve the people using the product
under pressure. When colours, words and states each mean one thing everywhere,
and the codebase enforces that, a product can grow quickly and still feel like
one product.
